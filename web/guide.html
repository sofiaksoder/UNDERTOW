How Undertow works, and how to use it

Markets behave like ocean currents: sometimes calm, sometimes jumpy, and sometimes pulled by hidden undercurrents. Undertow is a live demo that infers the hidden current from nothing but daily price moves, on simulated markets and on real SPY data, and lets you adjust the key factors.

01What it is

A volatility detector you can watch in real time

Volatility is how wildly a price swings. A calm stock might move 0.5% on a typical day; in extreme events the same stock can move 5%.

The catch: you can never observe volatility directly, only prices. A big move today might mean the market has become jumpy, or it might be one random stagger in a calm market. Telling the two apart is the problem Undertow solves.

Undertow uses a Monte Carlo method called particle filtering. It runs entirely in your browser and shows every step: the guesses, how they’re scored, and the conclusion it reaches each day.

02The idea

The surface and the undertow

Think of the sea. On the surface you see waves: the daily returns, the ups and downs of the price. Underneath is the undertow, a current you can’t see that decides how big the waves tend to be. That current is volatility. A strong current means big waves; a weak one means small waves. Undertow watches the waves and infers the current.

Two simple assumptions power it:

  • The current changes slowly. Today’s volatility is close to yesterday’s, plus a small random nudge. Calm periods tend to stay calm; stormy ones tend to stay stormy. This is what lets the filter learn from past days.
  • Each day’s move is random, scaled by the current. The stronger the current, the larger the typical move. A large move is evidence of a strong current, but not proof.

A single day is noisy evidence, so Undertow never gives one confident number. It keeps a range of possibilities and how likely each is, which is why the screen shows bands and clouds, not just a line.

03How it works

Imagine sending out 1,000 scouts, each carrying a different guess about how stormy the market is right now. These scouts are called particles. Every day they repeat three steps:

STEP 01

Predict

Each scout moves forward one day. Because the current changes slowly, each guess drifts only a little, plus a small random nudge. The swarm spreads out slightly, reflecting new uncertainty.

STEP 02

Weight

Today’s price move arrives. Each scout is scored on how believable that move would be if its guess were true. A huge move favours “stormy” scouts; a tiny move favours “calm” ones. Good fits get a high weight.

STEP 03

Resample

If only a few scouts are doing most of the work, the swarm is wasteful. Poor fits are dropped and good fits are copied, restoring a full, useful swarm. This happens only when needed, and it is the core of particle filtering.

After the weighting step, the swarm is the answer. The weighted average of the guesses is the headline volatility number. The spread of the guesses gives the bands: where the swarm is tight, the filter is confident; where it’s wide, it isn’t.

Why not just use a formula?

Simple heuristics, such as the standard deviation of the last 20 days, are slow to detect changes and give no measure of uncertainty. A particle filter instead uses an explicit model of how volatility evolves, so it adapts quickly to moderate changes and reports its estimation uncertainty. It is slower after very large, sudden jumps unless it is given a way to expect them (see the scorecard). It also handles the model’s intractable mathematics, which has no closed-form solution.

Volatility is shown as an annualised percentage: roughly how much the price would move over a year at the current pace. Calm markets typically sit around 10-15%; crises can exceed 60%.

Using the controls

The demo opens on a title screen. Click Enter → (or press Enter or Space) to start; it then autoplays, unless your device requests reduced motion, in which case it stays paused until you press Play. Controls are in the right-hand column (below the charts on a phone).

Why day 70?
The first 70 days are warm-up (burn-in). The filter starts from a vague guess about volatility and needs a few weeks of returns to settle, and the sea-state surface needs 64 days of history to fill. In the real-data preset the warm-up is October to December 2018, and playback starts on 14 February 2020, just before the crash.

Good to know

The filter sliders change what the filter believes; the data sliders change the simulated market. By default they match (φ = 0.97, σ_η = 0.15), the best case. On real data the filter uses φ = 0.94 and σ_η = 0.35, estimated from SPY in 2010-2018. EWMA and GARCH use fixed settings (EWMA 0.94; GARCH fitted offline), so they don’t follow the sliders.

06Things to try
  1. Watch one day in slow motion. Press Pause, then press Step → a few times and read the caption while watching panel 04. Dots grow when they fit the day’s return, and the cloud tightens after resampling.
  2. Test the reaction time. Press Add a shock, then play. The dashed true-volatility line jumps immediately. Count how many days the filter takes to catch up, and compare with EWMA and GARCH.
  3. See the effect of particle count. Set Particles N to 100, then to 5,000. At 100 the estimate is jittery and the bands are ragged; at 5,000 it is smooth. More particles means less Monte Carlo noise.
  4. Misspecify the model in either direction. Set the filter’s σ_η to 0.05 while the data stay at 0.15: the filter becomes sluggish and overconfident. Then restore it and raise the data’s true σ_η to 0.35 instead: the market becomes restless and a filter that expects calm falls behind.
  5. Compare the scenarios. Switch between Calm, 2008 and 2020 and play each. After the large 2020 jump the plain filter lags for several days. Now tick Jump component and replay: it catches up faster.
  6. Meet a real market. Choose Real: SPY 2020 and play through the March 2020 crash. Compare the filter with GARCH, EWMA and the VIX. Then tick Jump component and see how the peak estimate changes. The realized-volatility line is hindsight, so read it as a rough guide, not the truth.
07How well it works

An honest scorecard

The figures below are measured, not assumed. The README has the full tables.

  • It matches theory. The filter’s error falls in proportion to 1/√N, where N is the number of particles. Quadrupling the particle count roughly halves the noise.
  • Its settings can be estimated. Fitting φ and σ_η by maximum likelihood on SPY in 2010-2018 gave φ = 0.94 and σ_η = 0.35 (the real-data preset uses them), well away from the hand-set 0.97 and 0.15. On the held-out years 2019-2024 the likelihood rose by about 9.
  • On simulated data that match its model, with no shock, it is clearly more accurate than GARCH (an industry-standard model) and slightly more accurate than EWMA and the 20-day standard deviation, though those gaps are within the noise.
  • After a very large sudden jump (+1.5 in log-variance, about a 2x rise), the plain filter trails GARCH by about three standard errors. Its prior treats such a jump as nearly impossible, so it takes several days to adjust, and adding particles doesn’t fix that. A jump component closes the gap: the jump variant is level with GARCH and better than the plain filter on 72 of 100 paths. It costs a little when no shock arrives.
  • On real SPY returns, out of sample (fitted on 2010-2018, tested on 2019-2024), it is significantly better than the 20-day standard deviation (Diebold-Mariano test, p = 0.003), borderline against EWMA (p = 0.064), and statistically indistinguishable from GARCH (p = 0.095, with GARCH nominally ahead).
  • In the March 2020 crash the jump variant improves on the plain filter (p = 0.036 on that window, which is only about 100 days). Student-t noise adds little on SPY.
  • The browser matches the Python. On the same data, the in-page filter and the Python implementation agree to within about 1% on average at 1,000 particles (worst single day: about 3-5%), consistent with sampling noise. This holds for the plain filter and both robust variants.
08Limits

Known limitations

  • Simple model. By default returns are assumed to be Gaussian, but real markets have fatter tails, so large moves are treated as rarer than they are. The Student-t option helps in principle, but the fit only weakly prefers it on SPY.
  • No leverage effect. In real markets, falling prices tend to be followed by higher volatility. The model treats return shocks and volatility shocks as independent.
  • Settings are estimated once. φ, σ_η and the average level were fitted on 2010-2018 on a coarse grid and then held fixed. There is no re-estimation and no uncertainty on them.
  • The jump settings aren’t pinned down by the data. SPY’s likelihood is flat across rare-jump settings, so the jump’s size and frequency are a modelling choice.
  • Scoring on real data is approximate. True volatility is unobservable, so methods are scored against noisy proxies. The significance tests allow for that noise, but the crash window is small.
  • The baselines are fixed. EWMA uses 0.94 and GARCH uses offline-fitted settings, so neither follows the sliders. The realized-volatility line on real data uses the following five days.
  • Not advice. The filter estimates current and past volatility. It does not predict returns or recommend trades.
09Glossary
Return
Daily percentage price change (log returns, nearly identical for small moves).
Volatility
Typical size of daily returns.
Annualised
Volatility restated as a yearly figure so values are comparable: daily volatility × √252 ≈ 15.9, where 252 is the number of trading days per year. A 1% daily volatility is about 16% annualised.
Realized volatility
The volatility that actually occurred, measured from returns after the fact. Here: the volatility of the next five days. It is hindsight, and a noisy stand-in for the true volatility.
VIX
An index of the volatility that S&P 500 options imply for the next 30 days, quoted as an annualised percentage. It reflects market expectations.
Hidden state
A quantity that exists but cannot be observed directly. Here, it is the current volatility, stored as its logarithm (log-variance) so it stays above zero.
Particle
One candidate value of the hidden volatility.
Weight
How well a particle explains the current return.
Resampling
Replacing low-weight particles with copies of high-weight ones, resetting weights to equal.
ESS
The number of equally weighted particles the weighted set is worth; near 1 if one particle dominates. Shown as a fraction of N.
Warm-up
The first days of a run, before the filter has settled. It starts from a vague guess about volatility, so early estimates are not reliable (also called burn-in).
Posterior
The filter’s belief after seeing the data; the particles approximate it.
90% band
The 90% credible interval; the narrower, the more confident.
Shock
A sudden jump in volatility. Shock days get coral ticks: the days you inject on simulated data, or days with a return beyond 4σ̂ on real data. Separately, returns beyond 3σ̂ are drawn as coral columns.
Jump component
An extra term that gives volatility a small daily chance of a sudden jump, making shocks plausible to the filter.
Student-t noise
Returns noise with fatter tails than a bell curve, so extreme returns are less surprising.
Rolling std
Standard deviation of the last 20 returns.
EWMA
Exponentially weighted moving average: today’s variance estimate is 94% yesterday’s plus 6% of today’s squared return.
GARCH
Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity: a standard model that updates volatility from the previous day’s volatility and return.
Out of sample
Tested on data not used to choose the settings. Here: settings fitted on 2010-2018, scored on 2019-2024.
Diebold-Mariano test
A statistical test of whether two forecasting methods differ in accuracy by more than chance. A small p-value (such as under 0.05) means the difference is unlikely to be luck.
φ
Persistence: the fraction of the previous day’s log-variance carried forward.
σ_η
Vol of vol: the standard deviation of the daily change in log-variance.
10Under the sea

For the technically curious

Model

The demo uses a stochastic volatility (SV) model. The latent log-variance h_t follows a mean-reverting AR(1) process, and returns are conditionally Gaussian given h_t:

h_t = μ + φ (h_{t-1} − μ) + σ_η · η_t
r_t = exp(h_t / 2) · ε_t
η_t, ε_t ~ N(0, 1), independent

Because η and ε are independent, the model has no leverage effect. The two robust variants change it slightly. Jump: each day, with probability 0.003, an extra N(1.5, 0.5²) is added to h_t. Student-t: ε_t is Student-t with 20 degrees of freedom, scaled to unit variance. Both sets of numbers were fitted on SPY.

Filter

The filter is a bootstrap particle filter with N particles. The initial particles are drawn from the stationary distribution of the AR(1). On each day:

  1. Predict: propagate each particle through the state equation (plus the jump, if enabled).
  2. Weight: compute the log-likelihood log N(r_t; 0, exp(h)) per particle (or its Student-t counterpart), then normalise the weights in log space for numerical stability.
  3. Resample: if ESS < N/2, apply systematic resampling and reset the weights to 1/N.

Per-day output: the filtered mean, the 5/25/50/75/95% quantiles of volatility, ESS, and the log-likelihood increment log p(r_t | r_1:t−1). The sum of the increments estimates the likelihood of the whole series, which is what the parameter fit maximises.

Parameter estimation and evaluation

φ, σ_η and μ are chosen by grid search on that likelihood estimate, using 2010-2018 only: a coarse grid with one seed, then the best cells re-scored with more seeds because the estimate is noisy. The 2019-2024 years are held out. GARCH(1,1) is fitted with the arch package on the same training years, and the demo then runs its recursion directly. Forecasts for the next day are scored with QLIKE against the next day’s squared return and with absolute error against the next five days’ realized volatility, and compared with Diebold-Mariano tests using a Newey-West long-run variance.

Demo implementation notes
  • Ridges: a lightly smoothed, weighted histogram of the particles on a log-volatility grid.
  • Particle strip: a snapshot of up to 280 particles at each step (predict, weight, resample) of the filter’s actual run. Resampling parent indices come from the real systematic resampling.
  • Caption: built from the run itself: the return in units of the previous day’s estimate, the change between the mean volatility before and after weighting, the change in the estimate, and whether resampling happened.
  • Fan chart: 60 simulated price paths over 20 days. Each path uses the filter’s one-day-ahead variance forecast and holds it fixed for all 20 days. The model itself lets volatility drift back toward its long-run level and wander randomly, so the fan is too wide after a volatility spike, too narrow after a calm spell (in the default model, roughly 20% too wide after a very large spike and 15% too narrow after a calm one), and has thinner tails than the model implies. Treat it as an illustration of plausible spread, not a calibrated forecast.
  • Real data: SPY daily log returns from October 2018 to December 2020, with the VIX. The first months are warm-up. GARCH’s recursion starts from its unconditional variance at the start of the window rather than from 2010, which only matters during that warm-up.
  • Baselines: EWMA (0.94) and GARCH are computed in the page. For simulated data the GARCH settings were fitted offline on long simulated paths from the default model; for real data, on SPY 2010-2018.
Verification

The Python package has unit tests for weight normalisation, resampling counts, reproducibility, the Student-t density, the GARCH recursion, the Diebold-Mariano test, error shrinking with N, and behaviour after a shock. The browser filter is checked against the Python filter on identical input, for the plain filter and both variants: the relative RMS error of the filtered mean is about 0.9-1.1% at N = 1,000 and about 0.4-0.5% at N = 5,000, and the log-likelihoods agree to within 0.1.

Source code, results tables, analysis scripts and the Python package are in the GitHub repository.
